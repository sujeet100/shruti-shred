## Alap and intro craft: research summary (Hindustani only)

The strongest source I found is David Clarke's *Rāgs Around the Clock* (Open Book Publishers, 2024, open access), https://doi.org/10.11647/OBP.0313, ch. 3, "How Do You Sing an Ālāp?". It analyses a 2-minute khayal alap in **Bhairav** by Vijay Rajput phrase by phrase and draws out 19 numbered rubrics plus timing measurements across 14 short alaps. Gayaki ang is modelled on khayal, so it maps well onto sitar. It is a vocal source, though, and the instrumental differences are noted below. I could not find verifiable verbatim statements from Vilayat Khan, Nikhil Banerjee or Ravi Shankar on how to shape a short alap. I have not invented any; that gap is flagged in section 2.

### 1. What makes an alap an alap

**It is unmetred and grows outward from Sa.**
- Clarke calls it unaccompanied, "free from any manifest pulse or metre—a condition known as anibaddh".
- Deepak Raja calls it "low-density melody, without explicit pulsation or rhythm". The **jod** "acquires a simple 2-beat pulsation". The **jhala** is "high-density melody … approximating a 4/6/8 beat rhythmic cycle" and "raises the first phase presentation to a crescendo" ([swaratala](http://swaratala.blogspot.com/2013/08/raga-performance-on-sitar-and-sarod.html)).
- The alap still has an inner periodicity. Clarke: "a stretchy periodicity whose unit of measurement is something closer to a breath rather than a beat". Sanyal & Widdess (2004) find a "subliminal pulse".

**It needs a plan, which Clarke calls a phase schema.** His teacher once asked him "What is your plan?" after a formless alap. The schema has three phases:
- **Establishing phase.** "1. Sustain Sā. 2. Explore the svar space slightly above and return to Sā. 3. Explore the svar space slightly below and return to Sā." It "confirms Sā as the embracing tonic".
- **Ascending phase.** "Improvise a series of phrases, progressing steadily through the āroh … until you reach tār Sā." The ascent can break off with a return to Sa and then resume, and any repeat ascents centre on "successively higher notes". Sanyal & Widdess link this staging to Śārṅgadeva's *svasthāna* phases of ālapti.
- **Descending phase.** It runs back to madhya Sa and "should be shorter".

**Each phrase has one held note, and it acts as a ceiling.**
- Every phrase is organised around a held **nyās svar** (also called the goal or organising pitch).
- Clarke's rubric 16: "do not generally go higher in your melodic elaborations than its organising pitch". The decoration comes "from below … rather than steal the thunder of what is coming up". A "discreet hint" of the next phrase's nyas is allowed at the end of a phrase.
- Phrases overlap: each one starts back inside the pitch space already opened (rubric 19).
- Widdess's term "internal scalar expansion": "a phrase is expanded by inserting material into it that reaches one or more higher pitches before regaining the original conclusion of the phrase" (Widdess 2011, quoted in [Field, AAWM 6.2](https://iftawm.org/journal/oldsite/articles/2018a/Field_AAWM_Vol_6_2.pdf)).

**Held notes dominate, and the pace is unhurried.** Clarke: "the prevalence of sustained notes during ālāp" is distinctively Hindustani, compared with Carnatic, which "places ornamentation much more in the foreground". His teacher's advice: "take your time, don't make it too busy."

**Notes are introduced one at a time.**
- Wikipedia's [Alap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alap) article (single-source, weak): "the notes of the raga are introduced one at a time … the first reach into a new octave can be a powerful event."
- An Imdadkhani sitarist ([raga.hu](https://raga.hu/en/raga-structure/), author unnamed) says the phrases "invocate the particular note by omission first and accentuating it eventually". In the sitar version, the opening descent runs from Sa to mandra Sa first, then the middle and upper octaves follow, with a **mohra** between octaves.

**Mohra.** "A short, gently-pulsed 'separation phrase' used to distinguish different sections of the alap" ([Ragajunglism glossary](https://ragajunglism.org/ragas/glossary/)). In Vilayat Khan's Todi alap, the pitch range "is expanded, punctuated by moharas" ([Skidmore](https://thompsong.domains.skidmore.edu/dept/courses/mu309/Campus/2%20North%20India/2-2%20Alap-Gat/05-alp-gt1.htm)).

**The short alap (aochar / aucār).**
- Ragajunglism: "A brief 'precursor melody', sung just before the entry of percussive accompaniment".
- Clarke: aucār "lasts just a few phrases". Often only the establishing phase is presented, and the ascent is "often curtailed in order not to overly delay the entrance of the bandiś". In some short alaps the ascent stops at Pa (rubric 13).
- In a short alap the dip below Sa stays within "four or five notes below Sā", meaning the upper tetrachord of the mandra octave (rubric 10).
- Wikipedia (Raychoudhuri's classification) ranks alap lengths: aochar shortest, then bandhan, kayed, vistar.

**Measured timing, from Clarke's 14 short alaps (56 s to 3:27).**
- The ascent up to tār Sa takes **on average 77%** of the alap (range 66–92%).
- The descent takes the remaining ~23%.
- Bhairav: 1:27 up, 0:32 down.

**The alap prepares the composition.** Clarke cites his teacher's maxim that the alap "should relate to the content of your khayāl". In Padma Talwalkar's Bhairav, the ascent goes only to Dha and pauses on Ga on the way back. These "prepare the svar space for the same pitches in the opening of the bandiś".

### 2. Master sitarists (thin evidence; flagged)

- **Vilayat Khan / Imdadkhani.** His left-hand technique gave notes "continuity … for a much longer duration … closely following the vocal music". He also used "highly intricate … khayal ang murki-s". The earlier Imdad Khan alap was "relatively simple" with "drupad type of meends" (Ramprapanna Bhattacharya's [blog](http://ramprapanna.blogspot.com/2010/05/imdadkhani-etawah-gharana-vilayatkhani.html); secondary source, cites a note by Arvind Parikh).
- **Shahid Parvez.** "He'll hold a note, bend the string, and bring out three or four more notes … within the same fret". The bend carries "a note through the end of a melody and into silence", and he emphasises silence (Prasad Bidaye, [Substack](https://prasonik.substack.com/p/sitar-space-and-ustad-shahid-parvez)).
- **Not found:** verifiable alap principles in Nikhil Banerjee's or Ravi Shankar's own words. Arvind Parikh's NCPA listening sessions on Vilayat Khan's alap and jod exist, but I found no text from them.

### 3. Raga-specific guidance

**Darbari Kanada.** Notation below is Raja's and Parrikar's, where M = shuddha Ma (our `m`).
- **Character.** Deepak Raja: its majesty lies in "the ponderous deliberateness with which it has to be rendered". That comes from "the andolit … treatment of … komal Ga, and komal Dha – in the ascent as well as the descent". These two oscillations sculpt "the two phrases which virtually define" it: **(g M R)** and **(d n P)** ([Sruti](https://www.sruti.com/articles/columnist/ragas-darbari-and-gara)).
- **Low register.** Parrikar: "a poorvanga-pradhana raga, with most melodic activity concentrated in the lower registers". Rishabh is the vadi and an important resting point, and "the pancham provides repose" ([parrikar.org](https://www.parrikar.org/?p=2043)). Ragajunglism: "explorations tilted towards mandra saptak". Bhatkhande (via Skidmore): its best ranges are mandra and madhya.
- **Resting notes and kan.** Tanarang gives the resting notes (vishranti) as S, R, P in the lower register and S', P, R in the upper. Ga takes a kan from Re ascending and from Ma descending; Dha takes a kan from Pa ascending and from Ni descending. Dha is skipped in the descent: S' (n) d n P ([Tanarang](https://tanarang.com/wordpress/raag-darbari-kanada/)).
- **What this implies for a short alap.** I infer that it should open low and stay low for longer. The organising pitches would run roughly S → mandra n/P (with the andolit d) → R (as nyas, via (m)g~ m R) → P. Reaching tār Sa is optional, as Clarke's rubric 13 allows; he names Darbari as a pūrvaṅg-pradhan raga.
- **Discrepancy:** some sources put Ga's andolan in the descent only, or call Ga weak in the ascent (Wikipedia, raga.hu). Raja says both directions.

**Bhairav.** This is Clarke's own case study ([OBP ch. 3](https://doi.org/10.11647/OBP.0313)).
1. Sustain Sa. Rise to an andolit Re with a flash of Ga, and return to Sa.
2. A murki leads to a held mandra Dha, then back to Sa. These two balanced phrases last about 17 s and 12 s.
3. Ga, then Ma ("structural salience"), then a break back to Sa through an andolit Re.
4. Pa, then Dha with Ni ("acute sensitivity"), then tār Sa.
5. Descent through the vakra N–D–M–P–G.
6. The close repeats the G–Re motion three times before resolving to Sa.

Most notes can be held, "with the qualified exception of Ni". Parrikar adds:
- Pa is "skipped … in the arohi but assumes prominence … in the avarohi".
- Re is "rendered alpa and often omitted in arohi phrases".
- The andolita r and d are "indispensable".
- The cadence is "G M (G)r, S".
- Careful Ga treatment avoids Kalingada.

Sources: [parrikar.org/hindustani/bhairav](https://www.parrikar.org/hindustani/bhairav/); Tanarang gives the resting notes as S, m, P, d ([Tanarang](https://tanarang.com/wordpress/raag-bhairav/)). **Discrepancy:** Clarke treats Re as vadi and Dha as samvadi; Tanarang has Dha as vadi and Re as samvadi.

### 4. Intros in metal, prog and fusion (weaker sourcing)

- **"Love You To" (Beatles, 1966)** is the best-documented rock precedent. It has an opening alap where "the song's melody is previewed in the style of an Indian raga", in free tempo, before the gat section with tabla ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Love_You_To)). Note the tension: in pop, the intro **teases the theme**, while Hindustani practice **prepares the theme's pitches** without quoting it (Clarke/Talwalkar).
- **Sitar Metal** (Rishabh Seen) is a Hindustani-rooted project, but I found no description of an alap-style opener ([sitarmetal.com](https://sitarmetal.com/)). I avoided Carnatic-origin fusion such as Agam and Motherjane.
- **Doom.** The canonical intro is "Black Sabbath": "The bell, the tritone, the trill, the tempo" (Kerrang!, via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sabbath_(song))). Atmosphere and space come before the riff.
- **General craft** ([Riffhard](https://www.riffhard.com/how-to-write-a-guitar-intro/)): "build anticipation for the main riff"; "Start softly and gradually increase the volume and intensity"; make the transition "feel natural".
- **Context.** Léveillé Gauvin (Musicae Scientiae) found pop intros shrank from about 20 s to about 5 s between 1986 and 2015 ([summary](https://scienceblog.com/music-streaming-killed-instrumental-intro/)). A 30–60 s opener is therefore a deliberate genre signal.
- **Why the musician hears "intro".** My inference: an intro points forward to the arrival of the theme, while an alap is complete in itself (Sa → earned ascent → descent → Sa). It may sound like an intro if:
  - phrases lead toward the gat rather than home to Sa;
  - the range opens up too fast;
  - held notes are scarce;
  - there is no descent and closure.

### 5. A checkable recipe

1. **Sa first.** The first phrase centres on a sustained Sa. Every establishing-phase phrase ends on madhya Sa. The opening note may differ, but Sa is the first nyas (Clarke 1–3, 9).
2. **Shallow dip.** In the establishing phase, nothing goes below the mandra upper tetrachord (mandra Ma/Pa). For Darbari, allow mandra Pa, d and n as idiom (Clarke 10; Parrikar/Ragajunglism on Darbari).
3. **Ceiling.** In each ascending phrase, max pitch ≤ that phrase's longest-held note. One short grace touch (under ~300 ms) of the next nyas is allowed in the last note (Clarke 16; Widdess's internal scalar expansion).
4. **One new frontier at a time.** A new highest swara may appear only after the previous frontier swara has been held as a nyas. Successive nyas pitches in the ascent never fall, apart from returns to Sa (Clarke 4, 11; Wikipedia "one at a time"; svasthāna via Sanyal & Widdess).
5. **Overlap.** Each phrase starts at or below the previous phrase's nyas (Clarke 17, 19).
6. **Only legal resting notes.** Held notes come only from the raga's nyas set. Darbari: S, R, P (+ mandra P, n), with g and d held only with andolan. Bhairav: S, r~, m, P, d~, and never a held N (Tanarang; Parrikar; Clarke; Raja).
7. **Peak late, descent short.** The highest note arrives at 65–90% of the alap (Clarke's measured mean is 77%), and the descent takes ≤35%. A short alap may stop at Pa, which suits Darbari (Clarke 13, Fig. 3.2.10).
8. **At least one intermediate return.** The alap returns to madhya Sa at least once before the peak (Clarke 5–6, 11).
9. **Mostly held, no beat.** At least ~50% of the duration is in notes held ≥1 s. Phrases are separated by breath-length rests. Onsets show no steady grid (high inter-onset variance). Darbari uses slow meend and andolan, not murki or khatka (Clarke; Raja; Wikipedia on Darbari, a weak source). The 50% and 1 s thresholds are my inference.
10. **Close and hand off.** End with the raga's cadence onto madhya Sa: Bhairav G M (G)r…S with the G–r repeated; Darbari (m)g~ m R S or d n P → S. The alap's last nyas pitches should include the gat head's first or sam note. Prepare it; don't quote the head (Clarke/Talwalkar; Parrikar; Raja). Optionally, a short pulsed mohra-like cadence can bridge to the band (Ragajunglism; Skidmore).

**Open uncertainties:** the master sitarists' own words; the Bhairav vadi; Darbari's Ga andolan in the ascent; and how far Clarke's vocal-khayal rubrics transfer to Imdadkhani sitar, where raga.hu describes the mandra opening first.
